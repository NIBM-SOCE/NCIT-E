Welcome to NCIT-E. You are already a professional: some of you came here with an ICT or related degree, some with a National Diploma in Teaching, and many with years in front of a class. NCIT-E does not start you again from nothing. It adds a second competence to the one you already have, and it certifies what you can then do.

This first session sets up everything that follows. It answers four questions: what the award certifies, why teaching ICT is a job in its own right, where your teaching sits in the national system, and where you stand now.

## 1. What NCIT-E certifies

Every award should be able to say, in one sentence, what its holders can do. This is NCIT-E's {% include term.html t="scope-of-practice" %}:

<div class="scope" markdown="0">
  <span class="label">THE NCIT-E SCOPE OF PRACTICE</span>
  <p>An NCIT-E holder can plan, teach, and assess ICT lessons, including in classrooms without a functioning computer laboratory, and can identify and address the misconceptions learners commonly form about ICT concepts. Competence was evidenced at the grade band recorded on the transcript.</p>
</div>

Read it slowly, because every unit and every assessment comes from it. It makes three claims:

1. **A lesson.** You can plan it, teach it and assess it. Not a whole course: that is the next level, NCIT-A.
2. **Without a laboratory.** Most ICT classrooms in Sri Lanka do not have a reliable lab, reliable power, or a device for every learner. A credential that assumed they did would certify something most holders could not use. So every content unit has a version that runs on chalk and paper, one phone, or no power.
3. **Misconceptions.** You can find out what a learner wrongly believes about an ICT concept, and teach so that the belief changes.

The second sentence matters too. NCIT-E is open to teachers from Grade 6 to A/L. The award certifies your teaching competence, and your transcript records the {% include term.html t="evidence-band" %} in which you showed it.

<div class="box key" markdown="1">
NCIT-E certifies **pedagogical competence**: the ability to teach ICT well. It does not certify that you have mastered the content of every grade. The skills you build here, such as finding a misconception and designing a lesson around it, work at Grade 7 and at A/L. What changes between bands is the depth of the content they sit inside.
</div>

<div class="qc" data-answer="b" markdown="0">
  <p class="qc-q">A teacher completes NCIT-E with an A2 lesson package and A3 micro-teach drawn from their Grade 8 class. What does the evidence band on their transcript record?</p>
  <ul class="qc-opts">

  <div class="qc-why"><p>Answer: (b). The evidence band is a field on the transcript that records where the competence was shown. It is not a separate award, and it does not limit what you may teach.</p></div>
</div>

## 2. Teaching ICT is its own job

Knowing ICT and teaching ICT are different skills. A strong programmer can still lose a Grade 8 class in five minutes, and a strong teacher can still be unsure how a variable works. What a good ICT teacher has is {% include term.html t="pedagogical-content-knowledge" %}: the content, joined to knowledge of how learners come to understand it.

Three things make this especially true in ICT.

**Learners arrive with working models, and many are wrong.** Your learners use phones every day, so they do not arrive empty. They arrive with ideas that work for them most of the time: the Wi-Fi bars are the Internet; the file lives inside the program; deleting a sent photo removes it everywhere. These are {% include term.html t="misconception" text="misconceptions" %}: sensible beliefs that predict wrongly. Explaining the right answer again rarely removes them, because the learner fits your explanation into the model they already hold. You need to find the model first.

**The content keeps moving.** Syllabuses are revised, tools change, and Sri Lanka is now in a reform transition (section 4). A teacher has to know which version they teach against.

**The lab is often not there.** A plan that needs thirty working computers is not a plan for most schools. Teaching ICT well includes teaching it with almost nothing.

<div class="box metaphor" markdown="1">
**A racing driver and a driving instructor.** The racing driver is the better driver. The instructor is the better teacher of driving: they know what new drivers get wrong, in what order to introduce things, and how to tell from a learner's hands that a mistake is coming. NCIT-E builds the instructor's knowledge, for ICT.
</div>

<div class="box breaks" markdown="1">
The comparison breaks in two places. A driving instructor's content hardly changes, while ICT content changes with every syllabus revision. And a driving instructor does not need to drive better than a racing driver, while an ICT teacher does need to know the content well: at least one level deeper than they teach, so that an unexpected question does not send them back to the textbook. Pedagogy does not replace content. It is added to it.
</div>

<div class="box task" markdown="1">
Think of one ICT concept your learners got wrong even after you explained it clearly. Write down **what they seemed to believe**, not what they got wrong. Keep it: you will need it in Unit M2.
</div>

## 3. The national ICT pathway

ICT enters the school curriculum at Grade 6 and builds through to A/L. There is no formal ICT syllabus below Grade 6, which is why NCIT-E's band starts there.

| Stage | Grades | Character | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foundation | 6 to 9 | Progressive ICT competencies: practical use, computing concepts, programming exposure, Internet and web skills | Curriculum subject; not a G.C.E. subject |
| G.C.E. O/L | 10 to 11 | Computer systems, data representation, logic, operating systems, productivity tools, databases, programming | Formal G.C.E. O/L subject |
| G.C.E. A/L | 12 to 13 | Architecture, data representation, digital logic, operating systems, networking, systems analysis and design, databases, programming, web, IoT, project work | Advanced subject with a G.C.E. A/L examination |

Alongside A/L ICT there is **GIT** (General Information Technology), introduced at Grade 12 in 2002 with national examinations from 2005. It is taken by A/L students who are not taking ICT. It is a high-volume subject and is often assigned to whichever teacher is available.

The pathway matters for planning because topics return. Networking appears in the foundation grades, again with more formality at O/L, and as a full area at A/L. When you teach a topic you need to know what your learners met before, and what the next stage will expect of them.

  <ul class="qc-opts">

    <li data-key="b">No: NCIT-E covers O/L and A/L only</li>

  <div class="qc-why"><p>Answer: (c). The band is open from Grade 6 to A/L. Grade 6 is the floor because that is where the formal ICT syllabus begins, not because of a programme choice.</p></div>

## 4. Syllabus versions: teaching during a reform transition

Sri Lanka is inside a 2026 to 2031 education reform transition. Reforms roll out grade by grade, so for several years different bands are on different versions, and some documents in circulation describe a version that is not yet in force.

This is the position the programme records for each band:

| Band | Syllabus version |
|---|---|
| Grades 6 to 9 | NIE Grade 6 to 9 ICT material, published from 2018 (Grade 6). The most stable band at present |
| O/L (Grades 10 and 11) | **Unconfirmed.** Grade 10 ICT has been reported as revised in 2026, while the reform roadmap indicates Grade 10 implementation in 2028. The two reports have not been reconciled, so the programme treats the O/L version as unconfirmed until NIE confirms which applies. A Grade 11 revision is planned for 2027 |
| A/L (Grades 12 and 13) | The 2017 syllabus is the current published NIE syllabus |

The rule that follows is simple: **always name the {% include term.html t="syllabus-version" %}.** "The current O/L syllabus" does not tell anyone which document you mean. In a transition, a lesson built from it can mix content from an older version with content from a revision that is not yet taught.

<div class="box key" markdown="1">
**The version check.** Before you plan a lesson, answer three questions:

1. Which syllabus document, with its year, am I teaching from?
2. Is that the version in force for this grade this year? Check with your school, zonal office or NIE, not only with the latest document you can find.
3. Do my textbook and the past papers I use match that version?
</div>

<div class="box mixups" markdown="1">
- **"The newest document is the one in force."** A revision can be published well before it is implemented.
- **"The textbook is the syllabus."** A textbook follows a syllabus version. If the version changed and the textbook did not, they disagree.
- **"All grades change at once."** The reform is progressive by grade, so Grade 10 and Grade 12 can be on different versions in the same year.
</div>

This programme follows the same rule. Every unit states the syllabus version it was written against. Until the O/L position is confirmed, the units build the Grade 6 to 9 and A/L material first and add the O/L extensions once the version is known.

<div class="qc" data-answer="a" markdown="0">
  <p class="qc-q">A colleague says: "I'm planning next term from the current O/L syllabus." What is the risk, and what should they do first?</p>
  <ul class="qc-opts">
    <li data-key="a">The version is not named, and O/L is mid-revision. They should check which version is in force for their grade this year before planning</li>
    <li data-key="b">There is no risk: O/L changes only when A/L changes</li>
    <li data-key="c">The risk is only in the textbook. They should buy the newest edition</li>
  </ul>
  <div class="qc-why"><p>Answer: (a). O/L is the band under revision, and an unversioned reference can mix two versions. The version check comes first. A new textbook may or may not match the version in force.</p></div>
</div>

## 5. How this programme works

### Hours

NCIT-E is **3 SLQF credits (150 notional hours)**. {% include term.html t="notional-hours" text="Notional hours" %} count everything a typical candidate needs to reach the outcomes:

- **60 {% include term.html t="guided-hours" %}** with a tutor: 45 in contact sessions and 15 in practical sessions;
- **90 hours of {% include term.html t="independent-study" %}** between sessions.

So most of the work happens between sessions. Averaged over 15 teaching weeks, independent study is about six hours a week. Some units ask for more: M2, which starts next week, carries 15. Plan your week around it now, not after the first deadline.

### Sessions

There are **20 sessions of 3 hours**: 15 contact sessions, one a week for 15 teaching weeks, and 5 {% include term.html t="practical-session" text="practical sessions" %}. Each practical runs straight after the unit whose content it teaches, so five weeks have two sessions. The order is fixed. If a session has to move, it keeps its place in the order.

| Practical session | Unit | Teaching week |
|---|---|---|
| Session 4: Accessibility and inclusive design (UDL) workshop | M2 | 3 |
| Session 6: Low-resource and device-scarce teaching practical | M3 | 4 |
| Sessions 8 and 9: Sinhala and Tamil digital content creation lab | M4 | 5 and 6 |
| Session 19: ICT learning environment observation | M9 | 14 |

### Attendance

Attend at least 16 of 20 sessions (80%). Practical sessions missed require a documented make-up.

The second sentence is there because three of the practical sessions, the UDL workshop and the two lab sessions, teach content that appears nowhere else in the programme.

### How the units are written

The content units, M3 to M8, all follow the same six parts, so you always know where to look:

| Part | What it gives you |
|---|---|
| Content core | What you must know, one level deeper than you teach |
| Curriculum locus | Where the topic sits in the syllabus, with its version |
| Misconception map | What learners get wrong, each paired with a {% include term.html t="diagnostic-question" %} |
| Teaching repertoire | Representations, analogies and activities, with where each analogy breaks |
| Low-resource variant | The chalk, one-phone and no-power version |
| Assessment repertoire | How to check understanding, and the marking errors teachers commonly make |

You have already seen two habits of the programme in this session. Every comparison is followed by **where it breaks**, because an analogy that is never broken becomes a new misconception. And every activity has a {% include term.html t="low-resource-variant" %}.

### Tools

Taught activities use free tools only: Moodle, LibreOffice, H5P, Scratch and the Canva free tier. No unit needs a paid licence, and every activity has a no-electricity or single-device version. The programme is taught in English.

## 6. The six strands and your baseline practice profile

Every NCIT level is built on the same six {% include term.html t="strand" text="strands" %}, adapted from the six aspects of the UNESCO ICT Competency Framework for Teachers. The strands keep their names at every level. What changes is the depth. At Essentials, they describe this:

| Strand | At Essentials, a teacher... | Notional hours in NCIT-E |
|---|---|---|
| S1 Policy & Context | Knows the national ICT curriculum landscape | 5 |
| S2 Curriculum & Assessment | Reads a syllabus, writes objectives | 11 |
| S3 Pedagogy & Learning Design | Plans an ICT-supported lesson | 65 |
| S4 Digital Skills & Tools | Operates the core stack | 48 |
| S5 Organisation & Safeguarding | Applies safe practice | 13 |
| S6 Professional Learning | Reflects; joins a network | 8 |
| | **Total** | **150** |

S3 is the largest strand by design: NCIT-E is 43% pedagogy, 32% ICT content, and 25% policy, assessment, safeguarding and professional practice.

### Your baseline practice profile

A baseline is where you start, recorded honestly so that you can see your progress later. It is not graded, and nobody expects high ratings in week 1.

**Rating scale**

| Rating | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1 | New to me |
| 2 | I do this sometimes, or with support |
| 3 | I do this routinely in my own teaching |
| 4 | I could help a colleague do this |

**For each strand**, give a rating and **one piece of evidence**: something you have done that you could show or describe. "I think I am good at planning" is not evidence. "I wrote the Grade 9 scheme of work for my school last year" is. If you have no evidence, the honest rating is 1 or 2.

## 7. Assessment at a glance

Assessment is in teaching weeks 16 and 17, in three components:

| | What | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| A1 | An invigilated objective test, half on ICT content and half on pedagogical judgement | 40% |
| A2 | A lesson package for your own class: a plan, a resource, an assessment item, a misconception addressed and a low-resource fallback | 35% |
| A3 | An 8-minute micro-teach of one concept | 25% |

To pass you need 60% overall, with no component below 50%. Full details are on the [assessment page](../../assessment/). Every session from now on builds towards A2 and A3, so keep your independent study work: much of it becomes your lesson package.
